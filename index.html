Growth over time

The field stayed small until the late 2010s. Output tripled from 11 papers in 2017 to 29 in 2022, and 2025 was the highest year on record with 56 papers.

Publications by research period (n = 342; *2026 up to February)
Why it matters. Publication volume is accelerating, but evidence of proportional industrial adoption is not. Mapping the field is both a scientific and a practical question.

Keyword co-occurrence

Five thematic clusters emerge in the Scopus corpus: classical SPC methods, general AI and machine learning, deep learning and feature extraction, process monitoring and fault detection, and industry context. Statistical process control (511 occurrences) and neural networks (278) are the most frequent terms, linked in 156 documents. Industry 4.0 and production engineering sit furthest from the methodological core.

AI techniques

Neural networks lead with 136 studies (39.8%), spanning everything from 1990s perceptrons to modern multilayer networks. SVM and PCA/KPCA represent the kernel era of the 2000s and early 2010s; LSTM, autoencoders and CNN reflect adoption after 2018; transformers, transfer learning and GANs are at the earliest frontier.

Multivariate problems drive the evolution. The shift towards deep learning and post-2020 methods concentrates in MSPC; univariate SPC and CCPR remain anchored in earlier families.
A dominant cluster. MSPC with feedforward neural networks and kernel/tree-based methods accounts for 27.5% of the corpus: the field has optimised for stationary multivariate problems with abundant labelled data.

Algorithmic sophistication is advancing quickly, but evidence of uptake in manufacturing remains limited. Model interpretability and statistically valid control limits for non-Gaussian AI statistics are recurring open challenges.

Six research directions

  1. Dedicated investigation of post-2020 architectures (transformers and transfer learning have only 2 studies each).
  2. Profile and functional monitoring, especially for pharmaceutical, food and semiconductor manufacturing.
  3. Validation on real industrial data, beyond simulators such as the Tennessee Eastman Process.
  4. Interpretability and explainability (SHAP, LIME, attention mechanisms), largely absent from the corpus.
  5. A shared terminological framework for “AI”, “machine learning” and “deep learning”.
  6. Characterising the mode of AI–SPC integration through a full-text review of the 342 studies.
The ambition is not only to map what exists, but to help restore the dialogue between theory and practice that Shewhart established a century ago.
